TRIZ Analysis for the Gentoo Antarctic Rover

2a. Identifying the Problem Parameters

TRIZ, the Theory of Inventive Problem Solving, is a structured method for resolving engineering contradictions. This analysis applies the TRIZ Prism to two product-development challenges in the Gentoo rover: balancing speed against energy consumption, and improving maneuverability without increasing weight or reducing reliability.

Problem 1: Speed vs. Energy Consumption

Gentoo is an autonomous, solar-powered rover designed for a 1,000 km Antarctic traverse. Its operating strategy prioritizes energy efficiency: the rover travels at an average speed of about 3 km/h and avoids unnecessary acceleration to reduce kinetic losses. This helps preserve the limited energy generated by its solar array.

The contradiction is that this efficient driving behavior also makes Gentoo less responsive in short-range situations. Obstacles, crevasses, steep slopes, and uneven terrain may require faster acceleration or sharper control responses. Increasing speed and responsiveness would improve local navigation, but it would also raise energy consumption and reduce long-distance mission efficiency.

Problem 2: Turning Radius vs. Weight and Reliability

Gentoo's large turning radius limits its ability to maneuver in narrow or obstacle-filled areas. A smaller turning radius would improve operational flexibility and help the rover adapt to difficult terrain. However, many steering solutions require additional hardware, such as differential steering modules or more complex steering mechanisms.

Adding hardware increases mass, energy demand, and mechanical complexity. It also introduces new failure points. Because Gentoo operates in Antarctica without practical access to repair, reliability is a core design constraint.

2b. Categorization of Problem Parameters and Contradictions

The first contradiction concerns energy-efficient long-distance travel versus responsive short-distance movement. In TRIZ terms, the desired improvement is Parameter 9: Speed. The deteriorating parameter is Parameter 19: Use of Energy by a Moving Object. Gentoo would benefit from higher responsiveness when navigating hazards, but frequent acceleration would increase energy use.

The second contradiction concerns maneuverability versus the cost of additional steering capability. The desired improvement is Parameter 35: Adaptability or Versatility. The deteriorating parameters are Parameter 1: Weight of Moving Object and Parameter 19: Use of Energy by a Moving Object. A smaller turning radius would improve terrain adaptability, but conventional solutions may make the rover heavier, less efficient, and less reliable.

2c. Identification of Inventive Principles

For the speed and energy contradiction, the TRIZ matrix suggests several principles, including Principle 15: Dynamics and Principle 35: Parameter Changes. These are the most relevant to Gentoo because they allow the rover's behavior to change according to operating conditions rather than remaining fixed.

For the turning-radius contradiction, the most relevant principles are Principle 1: Segmentation, Principle 6: Universality, Principle 15: Dynamics, and Principle 19: Periodic Action. These principles support modular control, multifunctional components, adaptive behavior, and selective activation of high-maneuverability modes.

2d. Generating Solutions

Solution 1: Adaptive Driving Mode

Gentoo should use an adaptive driving strategy instead of a fixed-speed strategy. During open, low-risk travel, the rover would remain in an energy-saving mode and maintain a steady speed. When sensors detect obstacles, crevasses, slopes, or complex terrain, the control system would temporarily increase acceleration, steering response, and local planning sensitivity.

After the hazard is cleared, Gentoo would return to its normal energy-saving mode. This improves obstacle avoidance while limiting the total energy cost, because high-performance behavior is used only when conditions require it. The solution relies primarily on software and control logic, so it avoids major increases in mass or mechanical complexity.

Solution 2: Segmented Wheel-Control Strategy

Gentoo should improve maneuverability through independent control of the left and right wheel systems rather than through a heavier steering assembly. In normal operation, the wheels would work together for efficient forward travel. In narrow or obstacle-filled areas, the rover could slow down and control each side independently to reduce its effective turning radius.

This approach improves maneuverability while reusing much of the existing wheel-drive system. It limits added mass, preserves energy efficiency, and reduces the number of new mechanical failure points. These advantages are especially important for a rover operating autonomously in a remote Antarctic environment.

2e. Trade-off Analysis Using Conjoint Analysis

A conjoint analysis can evaluate the proposed solutions by comparing configurations across five attributes: energy consumption, operating speed, turning radius, system weight, and maneuverability. Each attribute can be assigned levels such as low, medium, and high energy consumption or standard versus compact turning radius.

Engineering evaluators would rank the configurations according to their expected feasibility for Antarctic operation. The resulting preference data would estimate the relative value of each attribute level and identify the configuration that best balances efficiency, responsiveness, maneuverability, and reliability.

Conclusion

The strongest solution is a combined control strategy: adaptive driving for terrain-dependent speed changes and segmented wheel control for improved turning behavior. Together, these solutions address Gentoo's key contradictions while protecting the mission constraints of energy efficiency, low mass, and reliability.
